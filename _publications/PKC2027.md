## Abstract

Modulus level is a critical resource in CKKS bootstrapping. Most recently, Yan et al. (PKC 2026) introduced the level-conserving rescaling (LCR) technique. The LCR method preserves one modulus level in CKKS bootstrapping by utilizing the small-norm property of ciphertexts after ModRaise.

In this paper, we further exploit the small-norm property of ciphertexts after ModRaise to save two modulus levels in CoeffsToSlots (CtS). The key observation is that the noise budget of key-switching on a small-norm ciphertext only requires the auxiliary modulus $$P$$ to be on the order of a single prime. The remaining primes that previously sat on the auxiliary side are therefore reassigned to the main moduli chain as a $$p_0 p_1$$ buffer above $$Q_L$$. With this buffer in place, we design a compact aggregated key-switching (compact AKS) for the first linear transformation of CtS, which lands the ciphertext at $$p_0 Q_L$$, and the second linear transformation then proceeds in the rescale-first style of OverModRaise to land at $$Q_L$$. Both linear transformations are therefore evaluated without consuming any prime of $$Q_L$$, improving over OverModRaise (Kim et al., CiC'25) and the level-conserving rescaling of Yan et al. by one additional level relative to each.

Secondly, we propose a lazy SubSum bootstrapping framework to resolve the problem that LCR and OverModRaise cannot be applied to sparsely-packed ciphertexts. Resolving this is crucial to the practicality of LCR, OverModRaise, and compact AKS. By proving that SubSum can be deferred until after CtS, lazy SubSum removes the key-switching that previously broke the small-norm property between ModRaise and CtS. This makes the level-saving techniques applicable to bootstrapping over sparsely-packed ciphertexts. The deferred SubSum is also evaluated at a lower level, which is more efficient.

Implemented in Lattigo, our techniques deliver a 21.2–32.4% throughput improvement on sparse bootstrapping on CPU. Porting the same optimizations onto an NVIDIA A100 delivers a 9.1%–10.9% end-to-end speedup on bitonic sorting.
